# Structure: what goes where, and why anyone keeps watching

Structure is the order in which a viewer learns things. It is decided before the first cut, and most weak edits are a structure problem being treated with effects. Lines here are [thumb] unless labelled. Nothing on this page predicts how a film will do. For short films built from generated shots, the story shapes and hooks are in `altocue-direct`.

## Start with one sentence

Before touching the footage, write: this film shows **who** how to **what**, so that **why**. If it will not fit in one sentence, the footage holds two films. Pick one. Put the sentence at the top of `brief.md`, and test every line you keep against it.

Then answer three things in a line each:

- **What should the viewer feel at the end?** Relief, urgency, trust, delight. One.
- **What should they know?** The single point they could repeat to a friend.
- **What should they do?** One action, or none.

## A promise, the proof, the payoff

Every film that holds attention makes a promise early and keeps it.

- **The promise** is the opening. It tells the viewer what they will get and why it matters to them, in words, in a picture, or both.
- **The proof** is the body. Each part either delivers a piece of the promise or raises the stake.
- **The payoff** is the moment the promise is kept. It must be the thing that was promised. An opening that promises more than the film delivers wins the first seconds and loses the viewer's trust in the next film.

Anything that is none of these is a candidate to cut.

## Openings

- **Open on the most interesting true thing.** The result, the surprising number, the mistake, the moment mid-action. Context comes second. A film that begins with a greeting and a name has spent its best seconds on nothing.
- **Say what is in it for them.** A viewer decides whether this is for them before deciding whether it is good.
- **Give the eye and the ear the same idea.** The opening line, the words on screen and the picture agree. Three different ideas at once is none.
- **Be specific.** "I lost 4,000 dollars on this" beats "money mistakes to avoid". A number, a name, a place, a moment.
- **Earn it honestly.** The opening line must be something the film really says and really delivers. Check the hook against the transcript of the whole film.
- **Find it in the footage.** The best opening is often a line from the middle or the end of the take. Move it to the top with `script.md`, then make sure the film still reads in order.

## Keeping the thread

- **Each part should cause the next.** [source] Trey Parker and Matt Stone's test for a script: between any two beats you should be able to say "but" or "therefore". If the only word that fits is "and then", the beats are a list, and a list has no pull.
- **Open a question before you close the last one.** Curiosity is an unanswered question the viewer cares about. Answer one, and a film with nothing else open is over, whatever its length.
- **Plant, then pay.** A detail set up early and used later makes a film feel written. A payoff with no setup feels random. A setup with no payoff feels like a broken promise, so if the payoff was cut, cut the setup.
- **Raise the stake as you go.** Order points so each matters more than the last. In a list, open with the second strongest, bury the weakest in the middle and end on the best.
- **Give them a rest.** Attention needs variation. After a dense passage, a lighter beat: a joke, a pause, a picture with no words. Constant intensity reads as none.
- **Change something when the content turns.** A new section earns a new size, a new place, a graphic or a shift in the music. The change tells the viewer that a new part has started. A change with no turn behind it is a twitch.

## Shapes that work for footage of a person talking

| Shape | Order | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Problem to answer | The pain, why it persists, the fix, the result | Advice, product, coaching |
| Result first | The outcome, how it was done, what it cost, how to copy it | Tutorials, case studies |
| The list | A count promised up front, items in rising order, a recap | Tips, tools, mistakes |
| The story | A situation, a complication, the turn, the outcome, what it means | Founder stories, testimonials |
| The myth | What everyone believes, why it is wrong, what is true, what to do | Opinion, education |
| Before and after | Life before, the change, life after, the bridge between | Transformations, launches |
| The comparison | Two options, one test, the result, who should pick which | Reviews |
| Question and answer | The question as asked, the short answer, the reason, the caveat | Replies, interviews |

Pick one shape per film. Mixed shapes read as rambling.

## Building from long or messy footage

- **Words first.** [source] Documentary editors call it a paper edit: choose and order the lines on paper before touching picture. In Altocue the paper is `script.md`. Mark the lines that could be in the film, group them by idea, put the ideas in an order that argues the point, then cut.
- **Then listen blind.** [source] Editors of talk call the next step a radio edit: the cut must work as sound alone before any picture is added. You cannot hear, so read the kept words in order, aloud in your head, as someone who has never seen the take. Every "this", "that" and "as I said" must point at something still in the film.
- **Then picture.** Only now decide sizes, inserts and graphics. Picture added to a cut that does not read is decoration on a problem.
- **Write the missing bridge as text.** When two good lines need a link the speaker never said, a short title or a graphic can carry it. Never build the link from words spoken elsewhere if that changes what was meant.

## Long form

- **Open cold.** The strongest moment of the film, or a one-line promise of where it ends, before any title. Then the title, short.
- **Tell them the route.** One line on what is coming keeps people who would leave at the first slow stretch.
- **Build it in chapters, each with its own small promise and payoff.** Write the beats in `beats.json` and `altocue deliver` turns them into chapters.
- **Re-open at each turn.** The end of a chapter names the question the next one answers.
- **Keep the visual interval honest.** In a long take of one person, change the picture when the idea changes: a size, an insert, a graphic, a screen. Tie it to the content, never to a clock.
- **End when it is done.** The last thing of value, one action, out. A long goodbye teaches the viewer to leave early next time.

## Short form

- **One idea.** A second idea is a second film.
- **The first frame carries the subject and the first second carries words.** Spoken, on screen, or both.
- **No runway.** Cut the greeting, the name, the "so today". Start on the claim.
- **Payoff close to the end,** so there is a reason to stay, with nothing after it but the action.
- **Make the last moment hand back to the first** when the film may loop: a last line that the first line answers.
- **Sixty to ninety seconds needs a second act.** At about the midpoint, turn: a complication, a second proof, a shift of place. A single flat run of that length sags.

## Endings

- **Land on the point, in the speaker's best words.** Often that line is already in the take, somewhere in the middle. Consider moving it.
- **One action.** Two requests get neither.
- **Stop.** The film ends on the last word's decay and a beat. Not on a wave, not on a second summary.

## Honesty is part of the structure

- **Never arrange true lines into an untrue claim.** Order implies cause. Putting a result straight after an action says the action caused it.
- **Never cut the caveat.** If the speaker qualified a claim, the qualifier stays with it.
- **A hypothetical stays a hypothetical.** "Imagine you saved an hour a day" must not be cut down to "you saved an hour a day".
- **A placeholder never ships as proof.** Numbers, reviews and logos come from the user or the speech.
