# Reading and choosing pictures

A shot says something before anyone speaks. This page is how to read what a frame says, pick between frames, and ask for better ones. Everything here is [thumb], the trade's working grammar, unless a line carries another label. You work from frames, so say which frames you looked at.

## Size is distance, and distance is closeness

The size of a person in the frame tells the viewer how close they are being let in.

| Size | The person fills | It says | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wide | A small part, the place dominates | Where we are, who is with whom | Opening a new place, an ending, scale |
| Full | Head to toe | What the body is doing | Movement, a demonstration |
| Medium | Waist up | A person at conversation distance | Most of a talking take, gestures |
| Medium close | Chest up | Attention on what is said | The default for a presenter |
| Close | The face | What they feel | The key line, a confession, a reaction |
| Extreme close | Eyes, hands, an object | This detail matters | An insert, a proof, a product |

- **Go wider for information and closer for feeling.** A list of facts read in a tight close-up feels intense for no reason. A hard truth told in a wide shot feels cold.
- **A change of size is a statement.** Moving closer says this matters more. Moving wider says take a breath, or look at where this is happening.
- **A crop changes size, never perspective.** A punch-in on a wide lens still looks like a wide lens. It reads as a second camera only when the source has the pixels for it, so check the source size before a crop past about 1.5.

## Angle, eyeline and headroom

- **Lens height sets the relationship.** At eye level the speaker is an equal. From below they gain authority and can look arrogant. From above they shrink. A presenter wants eye level, or a touch above.
- **Into the lens is talking to the viewer.** Off the lens is talking to someone else, which suits an interview. Pick one for the film and hold it. A presenter whose eyes drop to their notes has left the conversation: cover that stretch with an insert or a graphic, or cut it.
- **Give a look room to travel.** Someone looking to the right of the frame sits left of centre. Space behind the head and none in front reads as trapped.
- **Eyes sit about a third of the way down.** The closer the shot, the less headroom. A close-up may lose the top of the hair. It never loses the chin.
- **Direct address in a tall frame sits on the centre line,** with the lower third left for captions and the app's own buttons.

## Where the eye goes

A viewer looks at one thing at a time. In rough order, the eye goes to: text, faces and above all eyes, anything moving, the brightest area, the sharpest area, the most saturated colour.

- **One focal point per shot.** If text, a face and a moving graphic all compete, the viewer reads none of them well.
- **Text wins, so text costs.** Words on screen take the eye off the face for as long as they take to read. Put them up when the face can spare it.
- **Depth makes a frame look expensive.** Something near, the subject, and a background that falls away. A person flat against a wall looks like a passport photo. Ask for distance from the background before asking for better light.
- **Separation is the real job of light.** The subject should differ from the background in brightness, colour or sharpness. A rim of light from behind does it. So does a darker background.
- **Leave a planned empty zone.** A frame that is full everywhere has no room for a caption or a graphic. Name the zone before the shoot or before generating.

## Light, read from a frame

- **Soft light flatters, hard light dramatises.** A window or a large source gives soft shadow edges. A bare bulb or direct sun gives hard ones.
- **The key comes from the side and above,** roughly 45 degrees each way, so the face has shape. Light from the camera's own position flattens it. Light from below is a horror film.
- **Contrast is mood.** Bright and even reads as open and friendly. Deep shadow reads as serious or dramatic. Match it to the subject before reaching for a grade.
- **Eyes need a light in them.** A small reflection of the key in each eye makes a face look alive. Dead eyes are a lighting fault no grade fixes.
- **Mixed colour temperature looks like an accident** unless it is clearly designed, such as a warm lamp behind a daylit face. One white balance for the face.

## Movement means something

- **A push in says this is becoming important.** A pull out reveals context or lets the viewer leave. A pan or tilt follows or reveals. Handheld says now and here. A locked frame says calm and control.
- **A move has a start, a reason and an end.** A drift with no destination reads as a tool left on.
- **In the edit, a push is `zoom_from` and `zoom_to` on a segment.** Keep it slow and small, about 1.0 to 1.06 across a line. Use it for the line that matters, not for every line.

## Sequences: how shots sit next to each other

- **Change enough or do not change.** A cut between two shots of the same subject needs a clear change of size or of angle, about 30 degrees or more. A small change reads as a mistake: this is why a punch-in under about 1.1 looks like a glitch and 1.12 or more looks like a choice.
- **Keep everyone on their side.** Two people talking face each other across the frame. Hold the camera on one side of the line between them, or they will seem to swap places and face the same way.
- **Direction persists.** Something that leaves frame right comes in frame left. Reversing it says it turned back.
- **Establish, then go in.** Show where, then who, then the detail. Or open on the detail as a hook and widen to explain it. Both work. Staying at one size for a whole film does not.
- **Cover an action from the inside.** For someone doing a task: hands close, the face, a medium of both, over the shoulder, then one unexpected angle. Five shots of ten seconds each cut into a sequence that a single wide never gives.
- **Hold a shot as long as it has something new to give.** A busy wide needs longer than a simple close-up. A shot that has been read is over.

## A presenter take, specifically

- **One sharp take gives two sizes.** The full frame and a punch-in behave like two cameras. Move between them on a new thought, on the line that needs weight, or to hide a jump. Never on a timer.
- **Come back out on a new section,** so the punch-in keeps its meaning.
- **Hold the eyes in place across a punch-in.** If the eyes jump on screen at the cut, the viewer's own eyes have to hunt for them. Crop around the eyes, not the centre of the frame.
- **Cut away for a reason.** An insert shows the thing being named, proves a claim, or covers a look away from the lens. See `altocue-broll`.

## Choosing a frame or a take

For a thumbnail, a freeze or a still of the speaker, look at the candidates and pick on this order:

1. **Eyes open and on the lens.** Never mid-blink, never looking down.
2. **Mouth closed or in a soft smile.** A mouth caught mid-syllable looks wrong at any size.
3. **Sharp, with no motion blur on the face.**
4. **Head upright and inside the frame,** with no hand crossing it.

For a take of a line said more than once:

- **The last complete take is the default,** because people improve as they repeat.
- **Then compare delivery.** Eyes on the lens through the whole line, energy that matches its neighbours, a clean first sound and a finished last word.
- **Look at the first and last frame of what you keep.** A segment that starts on closed eyes or ends on a half-turned head shows as a twitch at the join.

## Notes to give before a shoot

Most problems in the edit are cheaper to fix on the day. Offer these before the user films.

- **Lens at eye level, and step away from the wall.** A metre or two of depth behind does more than any filter.
- **Face the window, or put the key 45 degrees to one side.** Turn off the ceiling light if it fights the window.
- **Lock exposure and white balance** so the picture does not breathe mid-take.
- **Film in the highest resolution the camera has,** so a punch-in stays sharp.
- **Get the microphone close.** A hand's span from the mouth beats any clean-up.
- **Record ten seconds of the silent room.** It fills the gaps the cut leaves.
- **Hold still and look into the lens for two seconds before and after each take.** Those are the handles the cut needs.
- **Say "redo" after a mistake and say the whole sentence again,** eyes back on the lens first.
- **Film the things you name.** The screen, the product, your hands doing the work: ten steady seconds each, then one slow move.
